Plastic particles smaller than 5 mm are now turning up in seafood, table salt, honey, tap water, and the air in our homes. A 2024 review in *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology* pulls together the evidence on where those particles end up once we swallow them — and the answer is unsettling: they accumulate in the gastrointestinal tract, resist digestion, and disturb the community of microbes we depend on.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

That community — the gut microbiome — governs digestion, metabolism, and much of the immune system. When microplastics tip its balance toward harmful bacteria, the review links the resulting disruption to inflammation, and to chronic conditions reaching well beyond the gut.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

This is not a settled science of proven cause and effect in humans. It is an emerging picture, built largely on animal models, with a handful of striking human findings. Here is what that picture currently shows.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Microplastics are plastic particles under 5 mm; humans are exposed mainly by ingestion and inhalation.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />
- Once swallowed, they resist digestion, linger in the gut, and can drive dysbiosis — an imbalance between beneficial and harmful bacteria.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />
- Dysbiosis is tied to a "leaky gut," systemic inflammation, and a range of chronic diseases.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />
- Much of the direct evidence comes from animal and cell studies; human data are still limited but growing.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />
- Practical exposure reduction — filtering water, cutting single-use plastic, and supporting gut health — is what the review suggests while research catches up.
</KeyTakeaways>

## How much plastic are we actually eating?

The scale of exposure is the part most people underestimate. Drawing on published estimates, the review reports that humans consume at least 50,000 microplastic particles a year through the infiltrated food chain, drinking water, and air — and that figure rises to between 74,000 and 121,000 particles annually when both oral intake and inhalation are counted.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

Drinking water in plastic bottles stands out as a leading source.

<StatBlock value="~130,000" label="Microplastic fragments potentially ingested per year from drinking bottled water" sourceId="Bora et al., 2024" />

The review notes that bottled water may deliver around 130,000 microplastic fragments to the body annually, that levels in bottled water are roughly double those in tap water, and that infiltration worsens when a bottle sits in direct sunlight.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" /> Sea salt is another documented carrier: the review cites a figure of 212 microplastic particles per kilogram of sea salt.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

## Why the gut is the front line

Microplastics are built to last. Once ingested, they resist digestion and can persist in the gastrointestinal tract for a long time.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" /> Animal-model studies described in the review report that the particles physically interact with the gut lining, causing mechanical injury and potentially triggering inflammatory responses. They can also increase intestinal permeability — the "leaky gut" that lets harmful particles cross into the bloodstream.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

The gut microbiome — the review calls it the body's "second genome" — is especially vulnerable. Multiple studies it summarizes link microplastic exposure to dysbiosis, a harmful imbalance between beneficial and pathogenic bacteria that can impair gut function, weaken immunity, and raise the risk of gastrointestinal disorders.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

The specific shifts vary by study and polymer. The review reports that polyethylene exposure increased the abundance of *Firmicutes* and *Bacteroidetes* in some work, while a study of infant gut models found polyethylene raised harmful pathobionts and reduced butyrate — a beneficial short-chain fatty acid.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" /> A systematic review of 28 preclinical zebrafish and mouse studies, cited within the paper, found microplastics tended to trigger dysbiosis by increasing *Firmicutes*, *Proteobacteria*, and *Chlamydia* while reducing *Bacteroidetes*.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

## From leaky gut to whole-body inflammation

The gut houses a large share of the body's immune cells, and its barrier integrity matters for immune balance. When microplastics compromise the tight junctions between the cells lining the gut, the review explains, bacteria, their metabolic products, and pro-inflammatory antigens can slip from the gut into the bloodstream — provoking both local and systemic immune responses.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

Two mechanisms compound the damage. The particles' sharp edges can cause microabrasions in the gut barrier, and the particles themselves can carry and release harmful chemicals — heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants — that add oxidative stress and inflammation.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" /> The review notes these toxic substances can drive the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, sustaining chronic inflammation in the gut.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

<Callout variant="key">
Chronic, low-grade inflammation is the thread connecting the gut to conditions elsewhere in the body. The review ties microplastic-driven dysbiosis and increased gut permeability to a pro-inflammatory state that is a known risk factor for obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and autoimmune disorders.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />
</Callout>

## The reach beyond the gut

The review's most attention-grabbing findings involve organs far from the digestive tract. It reports that microplastics can translocate from the gut to the liver, kidneys, and brain, and have been detected in human placenta, breast milk, testicles, and even the heart.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

On the cardiovascular side, the review summarizes a prospective study of patients undergoing carotid surgery: polyethylene was detected in 58.4% of patients' arterial plaques and polyvinyl chloride in 12.1%, and patients with detectable microplastics in their plaques faced a substantially higher risk of a major cardiovascular event — a reported hazard ratio of 4.53.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

For the brain, the review cites autopsy work indicating microplastic concentrations were highest in brain tissue compared with liver and kidney, with levels rising across all organs between 2016 and 2024, and polyethylene the most abundant polymer.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" /> A separate study it describes found microplastics in the olfactory bulbs of 8 of 15 individuals, raising the possibility that particles reach the brain via the nose and bypass the blood-brain barrier.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

## What the evidence does — and doesn't — prove

This is where honesty matters. The review is explicit that our understanding is still emerging, and that much of what we know about how microplastics reshape gut microbiota comes from non-human animal models, not humans.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

<Callout variant="warning">
The paper states plainly that there are few studies to date on how microplastics affect the microbiota in human guts, and that it remains unclear how much harm comes from particles accumulating in cells and tissues versus functional changes originating in the gut. Association is not proof of causation, and dose, polymer type, and particle size all vary widely across the studies summarized.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />
</Callout>

The human findings — plastics in plaques, in brains, in placentas — are real and verified in the review, but they describe presence and correlation. Establishing that everyday exposure *causes* specific chronic diseases in people will require far more research, which the review repeatedly calls for.

## What you can actually do

The review's own recommendations are practical rather than alarmist. On the exposure side, it points to using minimal plastic packaging, avoiding single-use plastics, filtering drinking water, and improving air quality as preventive measures.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

On the gut-health side, it suggests supporting the microbiome directly: a fiber-rich, prebiotic, and probiotic diet, along with regular exercise and stress management, to help counteract microplastic-induced dysbiosis.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" /> The review also flags emerging therapeutic directions — including fecal microbiota transplantation and specific probiotics such as *Akkermansia muciniphila* — but these are early-stage research avenues, not established treatments.<Citation id="Bora et al., 2024" />

None of this requires panic. It points, instead, toward reducing avoidable plastic exposure where it is easy to do so — starting with what you drink from — while the science continues to fill in the gaps.

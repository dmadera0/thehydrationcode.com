Most conversations about water contamination assume the problem is a single, named chemical you can filter out. The reality is messier. A 2026 critical review in the *Journal of Xenobiotics* describes surface water, wastewater, and drinking water as carrying **complex mixtures** of emerging contaminants and microplastics — often at trace levels, frequently alongside transformation products and particle-bound burdens that routine monitoring was never designed to catch.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

That framing matters. It means the honest answer to "is my water clean?" is rarely a clean yes or no. It's a question of what was tested, at what sensitivity, and whether the thing that actually poses a risk was even on the list.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Emerging contaminants (ECs) and microplastics (MPs) turn up in drinking, surface, and waste water as **mixtures**, not isolated compounds.
- Conventional water treatment was **not designed** to remove many of these contaminants, so they keep entering receiving waters.
- Apparent "removal" often means **transfer to sludge or soil**, not true elimination.
- Microplastics can act as **carriers** for chemicals and microbes — but this effect depends heavily on polymer type, aging, and conditions.
- Risk assessment is limited by **analytical uncertainty**: incomplete target lists and missing standards mean some contaminants are never measured.
</KeyTakeaways>

## What "emerging contaminants" actually means

The term sounds like it should mean brand-new chemicals. It doesn't, quite. The review uses "emerging contaminants" as a broad working category for chemical and biological stressors that are increasingly detected, often not fully regulated, and still need assessment because of their persistence, mobility, transformation potential, or toxicity.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

Under that umbrella sit pharmaceuticals and personal care products, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), disinfection by-products, endocrine-disrupting chemicals, pesticides, algal toxins, nanomaterials, tire-derived chemicals, and even antibiotic resistance genes.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

PFAS earn special attention. The review notes their strong carbon–fluorine bonds make them extremely persistent and resistant to both environmental and treatment-induced degradation<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> — which is why they accumulate rather than fade.

## The treatment illusion: removal often means relocation

Here's the point most product marketing skips. Conventional wastewater and drinking water treatment processes were not originally designed to remove many of these contaminants, which is a core reason they keep reaching receiving waters and, in some cases, drinking water supplies.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

Even where treatment appears to work, the numbers hide a transfer. Many pharmaceuticals and personal care products — particularly hydrophobic or ionizable ones like triclosan and certain antibiotics — partition into sludge. As the review puts it, apparent removal from the water does not necessarily mean elimination; it can simply mean the contaminant moved to the solid phase.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

<StatBlock value="78%+" label="of influent microplastics removed from water by most secondary-or-higher wastewater plants — into sludge, not out of existence" source="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

The microplastic figures make the relocation concrete. Across compiled datasets, the review reports median microplastic concentrations of 225 particles per kg in control soils, versus 1,106 per kg in sludge-amended soils, while stabilized sludge itself reached a median of 18,923 particles per kg.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> In Europe, land application of biosolids has been estimated to return roughly 125–850 tonnes of microplastics per million inhabitants per year.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

<Callout variant="key">
"Removal" from water frequently means transfer to solids. When those solids become fertilizer, the contamination shifts from your rivers to your farmland — and potentially back into the food chain.
</Callout>

## Where the contaminants come from

The review traces multiple, overlapping source pathways rather than one villain. Landfills act as concentrated reservoirs, with leachates sometimes carrying contaminant loads higher than municipal wastewater.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> Wastewater plants function as continuous but incomplete barriers. Agricultural reuse — treated wastewater, biosolids, plastic mulch films — extends contamination into the soil–water–plant system.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

Urban runoff adds a different signature: sharp, episodic pulses. Tire wear is the standout example. In one Australian stormwater study cited in the review, tire-wear particles accounted for about 95% of all particles measured, at concentrations of 2.5–58 particles per litre.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> These pulses are the reason a single grab sample can badly misrepresent real exposure — storm-driven spikes come and go faster than routine monitoring can see them.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

## Microplastics as carriers — with a caveat

The scarier headlines about microplastics often hinge on their role as chemical "vectors." The review takes this seriously but refuses to overstate it. Experimental evidence shows microplastics can act as polymer-specific sorbents — polystyrene showed the highest affinity for cyanotoxins in testing, followed by polyethylene and PVC, while PET adsorbed very little under the same conditions.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> Smaller particles adsorb more, thanks to greater reactive surface area.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

Field studies have detected pharmaceuticals like ibuprofen, sulfamethoxazole, and carbamazepine on plastic particles, with acetaminophen reaching around 1,831 ng per gram on plastic litter.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> Plastic surfaces can also host microbial communities — one study of greenhouse polyethylene films found 295 antibiotic resistance genes associated with the "plastisphere."<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

<Callout variant="note">
The review is explicit that the vector role of microplastics "should not be interpreted as universal." In some settings microplastics enhance contaminant transport; in others they act mainly as temporary sinks, or contribute less than natural organic matter and suspended solids. Sorption depends on polymer type, aging, biofilm formation, particle size, and the specific contaminant.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />
</Callout>

This is exactly the kind of nuance that separates careful science from clickbait. Microplastics being *able* to carry contaminants is not the same as them *always* doing so.

## The uncomfortable part: we can't measure what we don't look for

Perhaps the most important theme in the review is a limitation, not a finding. Risk assessment remains strongly constrained by analytical uncertainty — incomplete chemical characterization, missing authentic standards, matrix effects, and limited toxicological evidence.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

Targeted methods like LC–MS/MS are the quantitative backbone of contaminant monitoring, offering sensitivity down to sub-nanogram-per-litre levels. But by design they only measure predefined analytes — they "cannot fully address the continuously evolving chemical space of emerging pollutants."<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> The review illustrates this with cyanotoxins: more than 250 microcystin congeners have been described, yet routine monitoring often focuses on a small subset dominated by one variant, MC-LR.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> A target list that misses most congeners can systematically underestimate the true toxin burden.

Newer high-resolution mass spectrometry approaches — suspect and non-target screening — expand coverage dramatically, catching transformation products and previously unrecognized compounds. But they carry their own limits: higher cost, complex data, and annotation uncertainty without authentic reference standards.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

The disinfection by-product field shows the scale of the unknown starkly. A database compiling by-products reported between 1974 and 2022 held 6,310 entries — yet only 651 were classified as confirmed, while 4,142 remained merely proposed structures.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

## Transformation: when treatment makes new problems

Treatment doesn't only fail to remove things — sometimes it creates them. The review stresses that transformation products form during treatment and environmental processes, particularly for pharmaceuticals, pesticides, PFAS precursors, and disinfection by-products. Some of these products persist, evade monitoring, and can be as toxic as — or more toxic than — their parent compounds.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />

In other words, parent-compound disappearance does not necessarily mean detoxification.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> A chemical vanishing from your test result may simply have become something the test wasn't looking for.

## What this means for you

None of this is cause for panic, and the review does not quantify a personal health risk from any specific exposure route — that evidence remains limited.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" /> {/* NEEDS SOURCE */} What the review *does* support is a more grounded way to think about your water:

- **Treatment is a barrier, not a guarantee.** Even good plants don't fully remove many emerging contaminants, and much of what they do capture moves into sludge and soil rather than disappearing.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />
- **"Not detected" depends on what was tested.** A clean report reflects the target list and detection limits used, not the full chemical space present.<Citation id="J. Xenobiot., 2026" />
- **Reducing plastic contact is a rational default.** Since microplastics can shed from plastic materials and can associate with other contaminants under some conditions, minimizing unnecessary plastic in your water's path is a reasonable, low-cost choice — not a cure, but a sensible reduction.

The larger takeaway from this review is humility. Water contamination is a moving mixture, monitoring is incomplete by design, and the most honest experts are the ones telling you where the uncertainty still lives.

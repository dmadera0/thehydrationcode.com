Microplastics are rarely just plastic. In natural water they act as tiny rafts, collecting other pollutants on their surfaces and carrying them wherever they travel — including, potentially, into the human body. A 2024 study in *Heliyon* took a careful look at what happens when two very different contaminants share the same polyethylene particle: the heavy metal cadmium and the widely used antibiotic cephalexin.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

The finding that should stick with you is not about the water. It is about the stomach. When the researchers simulated gastric juice, the microplastics released more of both pollutants there than in tap water or seawater.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> That is the exact scenario that matters if a microplastic is swallowed.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Polyethylene microplastics adsorbed cadmium more readily than the antibiotic cephalexin, with a maximum cadmium uptake of 1.8 mg/g.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />
- The smallest particles tested (30–63 μm) had the highest adsorption capacity, thanks to greater surface area.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />
- Both pollutants released more readily in simulated stomach conditions than in tap water or seawater.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />
- Cephalexin generally suppressed cadmium uptake, showing the two contaminants compete for the same binding sites.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />
- The health-risk math found cadmium's non-carcinogenic hazard for adults exceeded the safety threshold in every water sample tested.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />
</KeyTakeaways>

## Smaller particles, bigger problem

The study began by sieving polyethylene powder into different size fractions and testing how much cadmium each could hold. The 30–63 μm fraction came out on top, and it was chosen for the rest of the experiments because it showed the most significant influence on adsorption.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

The reason is straightforward surface geometry: smaller particles pack more surface area — and therefore more binding sites — into the same mass. The measured cadmium adsorption capacity for the smallest fraction was 0.379 mg/g.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

<StatBlock value="1.8 mg/g" label="Maximum cadmium uptake capacity on polyethylene microplastics" sourceId="Heliyon, 2024" />

This matters beyond the lab bench. Contaminants are more likely to be transported by microplastics of smaller size, and the 30–500 μm range makes up a meaningful share of the microplastic mass floating in the oceans.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

## Cadmium sticks better than the antibiotic

When the two pollutants were compared head to head, cadmium was adsorbed onto the polyethylene more strongly than cephalexin.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> The plastic's surface carries a negative charge in most natural water — above its zero-point-charge pH of about 4.6 — which pulls positively charged cadmium ions toward it.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

Cephalexin's behavior is more complicated because it changes its electrical charge depending on pH. It is cationic below a pH of about 6.88, zwitterionic in the middle range, and anionic above it.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> That is why the antibiotic adsorbed best at pH 6 and 7 and dropped off at pH 8, where its anionic form is repelled by the negatively charged plastic surface.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

Notably, cephalexin did not adsorb onto the microplastics at all in a seawater environment.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

## The two pollutants compete

One of the more interesting results is that the antibiotic and the metal are not passive passengers — they interfere with each other. Overall, the presence of cephalexin had a negative effect on cadmium uptake, and a concentration of 3 mg/L cephalexin had the greatest adverse effect.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

The isotherm modeling backed this up. The maximum cadmium capacity fell from 1.86 mg/g without the antibiotic to 1.70 mg/g when 3 mg/L of cephalexin was present.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> The two contaminants appear to compete for the same negatively charged sites on the plastic, with the cadmium–cephalexin complexes creating steric hindrance that weakens cadmium binding.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

The relationship is not perfectly linear. As cephalexin rose from 5 to 10 mg/L, cadmium adsorption actually increased, then stayed relatively stable between 10 and 15 mg/L before declining again as the antibiotic dominated the available sites at 20 mg/L.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> Co-contamination, in other words, behaves in ways single-pollutant studies can miss.

## What the plastic itself tells us

The researchers used several instruments to check whether the pollutants formed chemical bonds with the plastic or simply clung to it. The answer points to physical attachment rather than chemistry.

FTIR spectroscopy detected no new functional groups after adsorption, which the authors interpret as evidence that physical interactions — not covalent bonding — govern the uptake.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> The plastic's crystallinity decreased slightly after adsorption, and its measured surface area dropped from 1.0635 m²/g to below the instrument's detection limit as pollutants accumulated on the surface.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

<Callout variant="note">
Physical adsorption is a double-edged detail. Because the pollutants are held by relatively weak forces rather than strong chemical bonds, they can also come back off the plastic when conditions change — which is exactly what the desorption experiments went on to test.
</Callout>

## The stomach is the release point

This is where the study earns its "human health" subtitle. The team measured how much cadmium and cephalexin came back off the microplastics in four environments: distilled water, city tap water, seawater, and simulated gastric juice held at body temperature.

The percentage of desorption for both pollutants was highest in the stomach environment, with seawater ranking next.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> Release depends on temperature, pH, and surrounding ions — and the warm, acidic conditions of digestion favor letting the pollutants go.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

<Callout variant="warning">
A microplastic that looks relatively inert in a glass of tap water is not the same particle once it is swallowed. The study's simulated gastric juice released more of its cadmium and antibiotic load than any of the water samples tested.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />
</Callout>

The authors also note a hazard that exists even without any hitchhiking pollutants: polyethylene microplastics have been shown to inhibit the enzyme acetylcholinesterase in certain fish within 96 hours, in the absence of heavy metals or antibiotics.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> Ingested microplastics carry their own risk, and the adsorbed contaminants stack on top of it.

## How large is the actual risk?

The study ran a formal human health risk assessment for the ingestion of cadmium through drinking water, in both real groundwater and spiked distilled water, and for children and adults.

For non-carcinogenic risk, the hazard quotient for adults was greater than 1 across the samples — meaning non-carcinogenic effects are plausible in exposed populations.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> The carcinogenic risk from drinking the groundwater fell into a "medium risk" grade.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

<StatBlock value="HQ > 1" label="Cadmium hazard quotient for adults across water samples tested" sourceId="Heliyon, 2024" />

Adding microplastics and cephalexin to the samples did not erase the risk. The authors are explicit that after adding MPs and CEX to water, the risk still exists, and that the risk of microplastics themselves should also be considered.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />

## The honest limitations

Good research names its own boundaries, and this paper does.

<Callout variant="key">
This is a controlled laboratory study on one polymer — polyethylene — using deliberately spiked concentrations and simulated fluids. The health risk assessment considered only the ingestion pathway, not dermal contact or inhalation.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" /> Its own authors frame the results as guidance for evaluating combined pollution and call for future work on how microplastics plus additives affect aquatic organisms and environmental health.<Citation id="Heliyon, 2024" />
</Callout>

A single-polymer bench study cannot tell you the dose you personally ingest, or how weathered environmental particles behave. What it does well is demonstrate a mechanism: microplastics can concentrate a heavy metal and an antibiotic, the two can interfere with each other, and both are more likely to be released under digestive conditions than in the water they came from.

## What to take from it

The practical lesson is not to panic over one study, but to update how you picture a microplastic. It is not a neutral fragment — it is a surface, and surfaces collect things. The evidence here shows that a swallowed particle can behave differently inside the body than it does in the environment, releasing its adsorbed load precisely where it can be absorbed.

If your goal is to lower your microplastic intake, the most controllable lever remains reducing the plastic your water and food actually contact — favoring inert materials like glass and stainless steel over plastic containers, especially for anything warm or acidic. This study doesn't measure that intervention, but it does explain why the particles you do swallow are worth taking seriously.

Micro- and nanoplastics are no longer just an environmental problem happening somewhere offshore. A 2025 systematic review in *Toxicology Reports* compiles the studies that have now detected these particles inside the human body — in blood, lung tissue, stool, placenta, kidney, liver, and more.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

The same review lays out how the particles get in, what they appear to do at the cellular level, and — just as importantly — how much we still don't know. This piece walks through its findings, including the honest limitations the authors themselves flag.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Humans are exposed to micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs) mainly through ingestion, inhalation, and skin contact — ingestion being the best documented.
- The review summarizes studies finding MNPs in human blood, lungs, sputum, stool, placenta, kidney, liver, spleen, testis, semen, and blood clots.
- In lab studies, the particles trigger oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death, with smaller particles generally causing more damage.
- The evidence is strongest in cell and animal models; long-term human health outcomes remain largely unproven.
- Suggested ways to reduce exposure include filtration, swapping plastic food storage for glass or steel, and HEPA air filtration.
</KeyTakeaways>

## How plastics get into the body

The review identifies three primary exposure routes: ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

**Ingestion** is described as the most documented route, primarily through contaminated food and water. The authors note that MNPs have been documented in bivalves, crustaceans and commercial fish, sugar, salt, honey, tea, and bottled water.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> Nanoplastics smaller than 100 nm are singled out as particularly concerning because of their ability to cross the intestinal barrier and enter systemic circulation.

**Inhalation** is the second major route. The review notes MNPs are present in both indoor and outdoor air, shedding from synthetic clothing and textiles, building materials, and plastic products.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> Because of their small size, nanoplastics can reach the alveoli deep in the lungs and potentially enter the bloodstream.

**Dermal contact** is the third. The review points to cosmetics, body washes, masks, sunscreens, and toothpaste as sources, and flags a less obvious one: the mechanical wear of implanted medical devices — polyethylene joint spacers in hip, knee, or shoulder replacements, dental implants, and cosmetic implants — which can generate particles directly inside the body.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

## Where the particles end up

The review's most striking contribution is a summary table of studies that identified MNPs in human biological samples. The list is long: blood, cerebrospinal fluid, lung tissue, lavage fluid, sputum, saliva, hair and skin, stool, urine, kidney tissue, placenta, meconium (a newborn's first stool), breast milk, testis and semen, colon, thrombi (blood clots), liver, and spleen.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

In one blood study cited, MNPs averaged 1.6 mg/mL of the total measurable concentration, with polyethylene terephthalate, polyethylene, and styrene polymers among the most common.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> A sputum study of 22 respiratory patients found particles in every sample, with 21 different chemical compositions and sizes ranging from 20 to 500 µm — polyurethane being the most frequent.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

Polymer types varied by tissue. The review reports that in placenta studies all identified polypropylene; in meconium, PET was the most predominant; and in every breast milk study, polyethylene was the dominant polymer.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

## What the particles do to cells

Across cell-culture and animal studies, the review identifies a consistent set of toxic mechanisms: oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis (programmed cell death), immune dysregulation, and even genotoxicity (DNA damage).<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> The severity depends on particle size, shape, surface chemistry, and polymer type.

In lab studies using human epithelial, liver, neuronal, and immune cell lines, the review reports that smaller particles — especially those under 100 nm — cause greater oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction than larger microplastics.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> Polystyrene nanoplastics, for example, increased reactive oxygen species, reduced cell viability, and promoted pro-inflammatory cytokine release in intestinal and bronchial cells.

Polymer identity matters too. The review states that PVC and PS particles generally show higher cytotoxicity than PET or PE at comparable doses, likely because of their associated additives and higher reactivity.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> Endocrine-disrupting additives such as phthalates and bisphenols are flagged as contributors to systemic toxicity, particularly in the endocrine and reproductive systems.

At the molecular level, the review notes that MNPs activate stress pathways including MAPK, NF-κB, and Nrf2, and that mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA fragmentation have been observed in several models.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

## The systems most affected

Animal work summarized in the review links orally administered MNPs to altered gut microbiota, hepatic inflammation, and lipid metabolism disorders, with accumulation in the liver, kidneys, and even brain.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> Inhaled microfibers have been associated with pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis-like pathology.

For the nervous system, the review reports that microplastics can cross biological barriers including the blood-brain barrier, and that once in the brain they may induce oxidative stress and neuroinflammation — raising concerns about a possible link to neurodegenerative disease. But the authors are careful here: human studies remain limited, and they state plainly that neurotoxic risk assessment requires in-depth investigation.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

On reproduction and pregnancy, the review notes that microplastics found in placentas have been associated with oxidative stress, cell death, and inflammatory responses, and cites studies reporting an inverse relationship between placental MNP levels and infant weight and height.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

## The part most coverage skips: how weak the human evidence still is

This is where the review is most useful, and where it diverges from breathless headlines.

<Callout variant="warning">
The authors state that most existing MNP toxicity research has been conducted in environmental contexts, non-mammalian models, and in vitro systems. While it is documented that MNPs accumulate in human tissues, they write that relatively little research has been conducted on the damage they actually cause — and that information on exposure dose, duration, and influencing variables remains scarce.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />
</Callout>

Several caveats deserve emphasis. Many of the human detection studies involve very small sample sizes; the review explicitly calls for larger, more demographically diverse studies. Many cell-culture experiments used concentrations far higher than realistic human dietary exposure — the review notes one study found cytotoxic effects only "when exposed to concentrations much higher than the actual human diet."<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

Measuring these particles in human tissue is also genuinely hard. The review lists the analytical obstacles: ultra-low concentrations below detection limits, interference from complex biological matrices, high contamination risk from plastic labware and airborne particles, and — critically — no universally accepted standard protocol for sample collection and analysis. That lack of standardization makes comparing results across studies difficult.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

In short: the presence of plastics in human tissue is well established. A direct, dose-linked chain to specific human diseases is not yet proven. Both things are true at once.

## What the review suggests you can actually do

The review devotes a full section to mitigation, split between reducing exposure and reducing toxicity in the body.

For **reducing intake**, the practical suggestions are concrete: replace plastic food containers with glass, stainless steel, or ceramic — materials the authors note do not degrade or leach microplastic particles into food.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" /> They also recommend cutting processed and packaged foods, avoiding plastic utensils and kitchenware (especially when heating food), and using metal or paper straws.

For **air**, the review points to HEPA air purifiers to capture fine particles and choosing natural fibers like cotton, wool, or hemp over polyester and acrylic, which shed microfibers during wear and washing.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

For **water and food supply**, it highlights filtration technologies — membrane filtration such as nanofiltration and reverse osmosis, plus activated carbon filters and ultrafiltration membranes — as effective at reducing microplastic concentrations.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

<Callout variant="note">
The review also discusses nutritional strategies — high-fiber diets, antioxidants like polyphenols and omega-3s, adequate hydration to support renal filtration, and bioactive compounds such as melatonin, astaxanthin, and probiotics. These showed protective effects in animal and cell studies, but the authors repeatedly caution that the mechanisms are not yet well understood and that further research is needed before any of it can be treated as established human guidance.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />
</Callout>

## The honest bottom line

Micro- and nanoplastics are now measurably inside human bodies, and in the lab they do measurable damage. The strongest, most defensible move the evidence supports is reducing your exposure where it's cheap and easy to do so — filtered water, fewer plastic containers, natural fibers — while treating claims about specific disease outcomes as still-open questions. As the review's own conclusion puts it, further research is essential to fully understand the health impacts and to develop effective strategies to mitigate these risks.<Citation id="Toxicology Reports, 2025" />

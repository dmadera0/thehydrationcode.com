"PVC is not merely polyvinyl chloride." That line, from a 2026 federal review, is the whole story in six words. The pipe carrying your drinking water is a mixture — a backbone polymer blended with plasticizers, metal stabilizers, pigments, and residues, each with its own toxicity profile.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

So when microplastics shed from an aging pipe, what actually reaches you, and how much should it worry you? A review by scientists at the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR), published in *Toxics*, worked the numbers for a realistic scenario. The answer is more specific — and more reassuring on some points, more unsettling on others — than the usual "plastics are bad" headline.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A 2026 ATSDR review catalogued 476 plastic-related substances across 98 toxicological profiles, grouped into monomers, metal additives, non-metal additives, and chemical precursors.
- For a modeled PVC exposure, one plasticizer — DEHP — drives almost all of the chemical toxicity. Other components contribute far less.
- The calculated hazard index came out around 1, consistent with a low estimated health risk from the chemicals in that cured-product scenario.
- A major unknown remains the *physical* risk of the particles themselves — their size and shape — which the review flags as a significant data gap.
- Real-world exposure measurements are still scarce; the authors call the quantitative estimates uncertain.
</KeyTakeaways>

## What's actually inside a PVC pipe

PVC starts as a viscous liquid, with chlorine cross-linking the monomers to produce varying hardness and heat resistance. To get the properties manufacturers want, they mix in additives.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> The review lists the usual suspects: plasticizers like di-n-butyl phthalate or DEHP for flexibility; carbon black for strength and UV protection; kaolin, silica, and alumina for strengthening; titanium dioxide for white coloration; and dyes for ink labeling.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

To map this complexity, the ATSDR team built a data visualization tool drawing on 98 of its Toxicological Profiles. Those profiles covered 476 substances tied to plastics production, sorted into four roles: monomers, metal and metalloid additives, non-metal additives and stabilizers, and chemical intermediates and precursors.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

<StatBlock value="476" label="plastics-related substances catalogued across 98 ATSDR toxicological profiles" sourceId="Toxics, 2026" />

Across all those profiles, the health systems most often implicated were respiratory, neurological, developmental, and hepatic effects — each appearing in more than 50 of the profiles.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> More than 30% of the substance profiles touched these systems along with immunological, renal, and reproductive endpoints, plus elevated cancer risk in the respiratory and hepatic systems.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

## How particles get from the pipe into the water

Micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs) don't need a dramatic failure to enter drinking water. As clean water passes through aging plastic pipes, degradation causes particles to shed from the pipe walls, with the review noting that stagnant water conditions significantly accelerate the release.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

The review describes three main routes of contamination from PVC and CPVC pipes:<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

- **Leaching** of additives and residues — the main process — where chemicals that don't bind tightly to the polymer detach and migrate into water.
- **Permeation**, where underground pollutants like leaking gasoline seep through pipe walls (PVC is relatively resistant to this except at very high contamination levels).
- **Heat damage**, where PVC exposed above 140 °F (60 °C) — as in a wildfire or structure fire — can release carcinogens including the BTEX mixture of benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylenes.

<Callout variant="note">
The review frames PVC pipe degradation as driven by physico-chemical aging: brittleness, surface roughening, UV exposure, thermal degradation, additive leaching, and biofilms growing on inner pipe surfaces. Prolonged water stagnation leads to higher concentrations of leached particles.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />
</Callout>

## The headline: DEHP drives the chemical risk

Here's where the review gets specific. The authors ran a component-based risk assessment on a modeled scenario: a 10 kg child (roughly one year old) ingesting about 3 µg of PVC leached from an old or damaged pipe.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

They compared each component's dose against ATSDR's minimal risk levels (MRLs) — the daily exposure thought to carry minimal risk of non-cancer effects — to produce hazard quotients. The result was lopsided. DEHP, the phthalate plasticizer, dominated. Phthalates like DEHP typically make up 35% of PVC by weight or more, and DEHP has the lowest MRL of the common PVC phthalates — meaning it's the most potent of them.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

<StatBlock value="~1.0" label="hazard quotient for DEHP in the modeled PVC exposure — almost the entire hazard index" sourceId="Toxics, 2026" />

By contrast, the other components barely registered. The modeled vinyl chloride dose came out several hundred times below its chronic oral MRL; cadmium, several thousand times below.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Aluminum is so much less toxic that a particle would need to be 20% aluminum to match cadmium's hazard.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Summed together, the hazard index was "not much more than" the hazard quotient for DEHP alone — consistent with a low overall health risk for that cured-product scenario.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

That matters for anyone trying to reduce exposure: the review points at one chemical class — phthalate plasticizers — as the thing most worth caring about, not the entire periodic table of additives.

## Lead is the exception that breaks the math

The tidy hazard-quotient framework doesn't work for everything. The authors explicitly excluded lead, because — as the CDC has determined — there is no safe level of lead exposure in children.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Since the lowest measurable blood lead levels are tied to serious effects like declining cognitive function, no MRL for lead has been derived at all.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

Lead also complicates attribution: it's hard to separate lead from plastic sources from the many other lead sources in the environment. The review notes lead in some paints — which are themselves plastics — can run as high as 10%, far more than in most other plastics, and that lead–PVC paints are sometimes used to color imported toys.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> In one community ATSDR investigated, with older homes containing lead paint, 56.7% of children had blood lead levels above 5 µg/dL.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

## The honest caveat: the particles themselves are understudied

This is where the review earns credibility by naming what it can't answer. The chemical-toxicity math addresses leached molecules. It does not fully address the *physical* hazard of the particles.

<Callout variant="warning">
The review identifies a significant data gap around the role of size and shape in MNP toxicity. The breakdown of plastics "may still present health risks due to the physical properties of the particles, independent of their chemical composition."<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Nanoscale particles can more easily enter cells and organs, and multiple studies suggest the *number* of particles — especially those below about 2.5 µm — may matter more for health effects than total mass.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />
</Callout>

The authors also flag a structural limitation: ATSDR has not profiled any polymers themselves. The agency's profiles cover monomers and component chemicals, which help identify chemical toxicity — but once an item breaks down into MNPs, other properties of the polymer become important and aren't captured.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Their ability to cross the blood–brain barrier, and to act as carriers for other environmental toxins, is called out as an area in significant need of research.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

And the exposure numbers are soft. The review states plainly that quantitative exposure estimates are still scarce and that additional studies are needed for accurate estimates.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> The modeled 3 µg scenario is an illustration of method, not a measured population dose.

## What the standards already handle

Not all PVC is equal, and regulation does real work here. Under NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 — the certification standard for drinking-water system components — NSF tests PVC pipes for volatile organic compounds, phenolics, residual vinyl chloride monomer, and regulated metals.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Certified PVC pipes and fittings do not contain lead, verified via NSF/ANSI/CAN 372, and rigid PVC pipes and fittings certified by NSF do not contain phthalates.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Residual vinyl chloride is tested at least twice a year.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

The catch is geography. The review notes these safeguards are well established in North America and the EU — especially for children's products — but that lead and phthalates remain prevalent in PVC products, including drinking-water pipes, in many regions around the world.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

## The practical takeaway

The useful signal from this review is prioritization, not panic:

- **The chemical risk from certified, intact PVC pipe is estimated as low** in the modeled scenario, with a hazard index around 1.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />
- **If one thing matters most, it's phthalate plasticizers** like DEHP — concentrated in flexible, not rigid, PVC.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />
- **Aging and stagnation increase particle release.** The review ties higher leaching to old pipes and standing water.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" /> Routine use and flow, rather than long stagnation, is the condition the review associates with less release.
- **The physical-particle question is genuinely open.** Don't treat "low chemical hazard" as "no risk" — the review is explicit that size and shape effects remain a data gap.<Citation id="Toxics, 2026" />

This is a federal agency doing the unglamorous work of turning a diffuse worry into a ranked, testable question. The answer it reaches — focus on the plasticizers, keep measuring the particles — is a far more useful starting point than the alarm it replaces.
